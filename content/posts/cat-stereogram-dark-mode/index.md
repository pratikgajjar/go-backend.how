The image below is two white cats on black. Look at it the right way and the two cats merge into one, with both ears, both sets of whiskers, and the tail. By the end of this post you will have seen it on your own screen, you will know where your eyes have to aim and why a fingertip gets them there, and you will know how a pair like this gets depth.

{{< figure download="y" src="cat-stereogram.png" title="cat-stereogram" alt="Two white line cats on black. The left cat has an ear, whiskers, and a tail on its left side; the right cat has an ear and whiskers on its right side and no tail." width="auto" >}}

A stereogram is a pair of images, one for each eye. When each eye aims at a different image, the brain merges the two into one. This is a side-by-side pair, not a single-image random-dot stereogram like the Magic Eye posters, so nothing is hidden in it. Each cat has parts the other lacks, and the merged cat has all of them. It is also flat: the two outlines line up to within 2 px on a 7,016 px sheet, so there is no depth to see yet. The last section adds some.

# Two ways to merge a pair

To merge the pair, the left eye has to aim at one cat and the right eye at the other. There are two ways to do that.

## Looking through works only for narrow pairs

The usual advice is to relax your eyes and look through the image, as if at a wall behind it. This aims both eyes behind the page, so the left eye lands on the left cat and the right eye on the right cat. It works only when the cats are closer together than your eyes, which for most adults are about 63 mm apart.[^ipd] Printed on A4, these cats are 106 mm apart. Looking through them would need the eyes to turn outward, which they barely can.

## Crossing works at any spacing

Cross your eyes instead, so the lines of sight meet in front of the page. Now the left eye lands on the right cat and the right eye on the left cat, and that works however far apart the cats are. The hard part is knowing how far to cross. A fingertip solves that: it gives both eyes one point to look at, and you move it until the cats line up.

Try it on the pair below. The questions under it tell you what to do next.

Yours will differ a little." >}}

With the printed card, the steps are the same:

1. Begin by placing your index finger or the tip of a ballpoint pen very close to the stereogram.
2. Gradually move your finger or pen tip closer to your eyes. Keep your eyes on the fingertip, not on the cats.
3. As the finger moves closer, the two cats behind it split into four. If the two tails are side by side, keep moving the finger toward your eyes. If they are one cat apart, you went too far; move it back. When the middle two land on each other, you see three cats. Look at a sharp corner of the middle one, like the neck of the cat.
4. Once the images merge, try to hold them while slowly looking around the head and body of the cat.
5. After merging the images, remove your finger or pen tip and keep the merged image. It sharpens once focus relaxes back to the page while your eyes stay crossed.

# Where the fingertip goes

The fingertip marks the point where the lines of sight cross. For eyes `e` apart, looking at cats `s` apart from a distance `D`, that point is `D × e / (e + s)` from your eyes. Answer each question under the drawing before it moves.

{{< viz kind="aim" title="Where the lines of sight cross" fallback="aim.svg" alt="Seen from above: the left eye's line of sight goes to the right cat and the right eye's to the left cat, and they cross at the fingertip" caption="Side-to-side distances are drawn 1.5 times wider than depth so the lines stay apart." >}}

So start with the cats close together: the fingertip sits further from your face, and the eyes cross less. If you cannot cross far enough, move back. The extra inward turn, beyond what the page itself needs, is the cat spacing divided by the viewing distance. For the print at 40 cm that is 10.6 / 40, or 26.5 prism dioptres (1 prism dioptre moves the aim 1 cm at 1 m). Doubling the distance halves it.

# Give it depth

The pair is flat because its two outlines line up exactly. A stereogram gets depth from small side-to-side differences between its two images. The pair below has its heads moved a few pixels. Guess first, then fuse it.

A stereogram made for crossing shows its depth inside out when you look through it, and the other way round.

# What it does for your eyes

Merging this pair makes both eyes turn inward together while focus stays on the page. Cards that work the same way, "Life Saver" cards and eccentric circles, are part of the office-based vision therapy in the Convergence Insufficiency Treatment Trial (CITT) group's trials.[^mop] In their 2008 trial, 12 weeks of supervised therapy helped children with convergence insufficiency more than home pencil push-ups, home computer therapy, or placebo therapy did.[^citt] The trial tested the full therapy programme, not stereograms on their own. I know of no study showing that viewing stereograms reduces eye strain from screens. For that, the AOA suggests the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.[^aoa]

# What to remember

- Merging a pair means aiming each eye at a different image. Crossed, the lines of sight meet in front of the page. Relaxed, they meet behind it.
- Crossing works for any pair. Relaxing works only when the pair is narrower than your eyes.
- The fingertip goes where the lines cross, `e / (e + s)` of the way from your eyes to the page.
- Depth comes from small side-to-side differences between the two images.
- The same exercise, with cards, is part of vision therapy for convergence insufficiency. It is not a proven fix for screen strain.

Try making your own pair: draw one outline twice, the same size and height, and give each copy a part the other lacks.
